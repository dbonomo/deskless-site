Claude for small business: how to set it up for a real team

Most small businesses that try Claude get a few people chatting with it on personal accounts, and nothing else changes. This is what I’d actually set up for a 5 to 20 person business: which plan, what it costs, and one afternoon of configuration that makes it useful on Monday.

Why not let everyone use personal Pro accounts?

Three reasons, in order of how much they matter:

  • Privacy terms. Free, Pro, Max and Ultra are consumer plans. Under the consumer terms, Anthropic may train on chats unless each person opts out (Settings → Privacy → “Help improve our AI models”). On Team, your business’s chats are not used for training by default, under commercial terms. That’s the one to have when staff paste in customer emails and quotes.
  • Shared work. Shared projects, organization-wide instructions and skills, and company-wide search are Team and Enterprise features. On personal accounts, everyone builds their own setup from scratch, and nothing survives when someone leaves.
  • Control. On Team, you decide which apps Claude can connect to, whether it can send email or move files without asking, and who has a seat. One bill, one place to remove someone on their last day.

Each seat is for one named person; Anthropic doesn’t allow shared logins. Team is paid by credit card only.

2. The afternoon of setup

The person who creates the Team account is its Owner. These are the settings that make the difference, in the order I’d do them. Most live under Organization settings.

Step 1. Write the organization instructions (30 minutes)

Organization instructions are up to 3,000 characters that Claude follows in every conversation for everyone on the team. This is the single highest-value setting, and most businesses leave it blank. Put in what a smart new hire would need on day one:

Example for a 12-person contractor

We are Example Roofing, a 12-person residential roofing company in Fort Collins, Colorado. We serve Larimer and Weld counties. Customers are homeowners, usually after hail damage, often dealing with an insurance claim for the first time.

When writing to customers: plain English, short paragraphs, no jargon without explaining it, never promise a date or a price unless it is in the information I give you. Sign off as the person writing, not as “the team”.

We never give insurance or legal advice; we explain what we see on the roof and what the work involves. If you are unsure of a fact about our prices, warranty or schedule, say so and ask me rather than guessing.

That last line matters most. Telling it to ask instead of guess cuts the confident-but-wrong answers that make people stop trusting it.

Step 2. Turn on the capabilities you’ll use (10 minutes)

  • Web search is off on Team until an Owner turns it on (Organization settings → Capabilities). Turn it on; it’s also needed for Research.
  • File creation (Word, Excel, PowerPoint and PDF files) is on by default, but its network access is off. Leave network access off unless you have a reason.
  • Memory: in Team accounts created since October 31, 2026, memory starts on for each member; in older ones each person turns it on. Fine either way. Just tell people it exists.
  • The feedback buttons (thumbs up/down): on Team, a chat someone rates can be kept for training. If that bothers you, turn “Rate chats” off under Data and Privacy.

Step 3. Connect your email, calendar and files (20 minutes)

Connectors let Claude read (and, if you allow it, act in) the tools you already use. Claude only sees what the person using it can already see.

  • Google Workspace (Gmail, Calendar, Drive) or Microsoft 365 (Outlook, OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams): an Owner enables the connector for the organization, then each person signs in. Microsoft work accounts also need a one-time approval from your Microsoft admin.
  • By default, sending or replying to email and sharing or deleting Drive files ask for approval each time. Keep it that way for at least the first month.
  • Anthropic’s small business page also lists connectors for QuickBooks, HubSpot, Shopify, Stripe, Gusto and Canva. Connect the one system people complain about most, not all of them.

Step 4. Make three shared projects (an hour)

A project is a workspace with its own instructions and reference files. Everyone who opens it starts with the same knowledge. Start with three, built around jobs people do every week:

  • Quotes and estimates: your price list, three good past quotes, your standard terms. “Draft a quote for this job from my notes” then produces something in your format, at your prices.
  • Customer replies: your FAQ, your warranty, two or three emails you were proud of. For the reply nobody wants to write.
  • How we do things: your handbook, checklists and policies. New hires ask it before they ask you.

Share each project with the organization. Name an owner for each one, who keeps its files current. A project full of last year’s prices is worse than none.

Step 5. Turn one repeated task into a skill (optional, an hour)

A skill is a saved set of instructions (and optionally templates or scripts) Claude uses whenever a task calls for it. When the same request keeps coming up, like “turn these site-visit notes into our job report format,” write it once as a skill. An Owner can then roll it out to the whole organization (Organization settings → Plugins & skills).

Step 6. Put it where people already work (10 minutes)

Install the desktop and phone apps for everyone. If your office lives in spreadsheets and documents, add Claude for Excel, Word and PowerPoint (generally available on paid plans), or the Google Docs, Sheets and Slides add-on (in beta on paid plans as of this writing).

3. What to use it for in week one

Pick work that is frequent, a bit tedious, and easy to check. These are good first candidates for a small team:

  1. The email nobody wants to write. Paste the thread, say what you want to happen, get a draft. Edit, send. Daily.
  2. Notes into documents. Voice memo or scribbles from a site visit or a call, turned into a quote, a job report or a follow-up.
  3. Long things, read first. A contract, a supplier’s terms, an insurance letter: “what does this commit us to, and what should I ask about?” You still read the parts that matter, but you know which parts those are.
  4. Spreadsheet cleanup. Messy exports from your scheduling or accounting tool, cleaned and summarized, with the formulas explained.
  5. Questions about your own business. With email, Drive and the shared projects connected: “what did we tell the Hendersons about their warranty?”

Then have each person name one thing they’ll use it for on Monday. Adoption fails when the setup is done and nobody knows what to do with it.

4. Five house rules

  • Check every number and every promise before it goes to a customer. Claude is good, and it’s still wrong sometimes, with total confidence.
  • No card numbers, Social Security numbers or health records in chats. Team is not set up for HIPAA; that’s an Enterprise agreement.
  • Keep approvals on for anything that sends, shares or deletes, until you trust how your team uses it.
  • One person, one seat. No shared logins, and remove seats on someone’s last day.
  • Someone owns it. One person keeps the projects current and collects what’s working. Without an owner, it drifts back to a few people chatting.

5. Dates to know

  • December 1, 2026: on Team plans, regular chat and Cowork (Claude’s agent mode, which can work across your files and apps and keep going after you close the laptop) merge into one experience by default, unless an Owner chooses “Keep disabled” under Organization settings → Capabilities. Decide before then, and tell your team what’s changing.
  • February 1, 2027: the separate chat and Cowork experiences are retired for everyone.
